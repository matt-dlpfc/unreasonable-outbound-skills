# The method

Our summary of Nate Nasralla's value driver tree, which he also calls the Driver Graph. Original: [Value driver trees in sales](https://www.natenasralla.com/resources/value-driver-tree-sales) and the "Driver Graph" one-pager on the same page. Read those for his full explanation; this file is only what the skill needs.

## The tree
A value driver tree puts the outcome a company is graded on at the left and branches it, right to left, into the inputs that produce it. Finance teams already run businesses this way, so drawing one makes you think like the buyer's executives.

| Level | What it is | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Outcome** | What the board or owners grade the business on: their numbers, their targets | Would the CEO say it on an earnings call? |
| **Engines** | The 2–4 things that produce the outcome: product lines, customer segments, channels, or the cost lines that eat margin | Do they add up to most of the outcome? |
| **Drivers** | Levers under each engine someone could measure next quarter: conversion, cycle time, attach rate, retention | Is there a number, or could there be? |
| **Behaviors** | What their team would do differently to move a driver | Is it a verb done by their people, with no product name in it? |
| **Pressure** (overlay) | Where something is late, behind, promised or changing | Is it dated and sourced, and on the node it hits? |
| **Owned** (overlay) | The 1–2 behaviors a given role can actually change | Would that person's job post or team page claim it? |

## The steps
1. **Start with what they're graded on**, in their words: annual report, earnings calls, investor decks, CEO letter. Private company: funding news, leadership interviews, job posts.
2. **Name the 2–4 engines.**
3. **Break engines into drivers.** Look for metrics they stopped reporting or redefined: that's often where the pain is.
4. **Find the pressure** and tag it on the node it hits. Changes often land on the left while the damage sits several branches to the right. (Estée Lauder, 2020: store closures hit retail sales, but the real damage was in shade confidence online, where shoppers could no longer try a colour.)
5. **Translate drivers into behaviors and circle what a role owns.** Trace that behavior back to the outcome and count the links: the further right, the more explicit each link must be.
6. **Map alternative paths**: other ways to move the same outcome, including doing nothing and at least one that isn't buying software. Each has an input it moves, a time to results, a cost, a risk, and an executive story ("a mix story", "an efficiency story", "a transformation story").
7. **Bring it unfinished** and ask "What did I get wrong?"

## Rules
- **Their numbers, not yours.** A number from their own earnings call beats your best benchmark.
- **Keep the product off the tree.** If the product shows up, it becomes a pitch and nobody corrects it. (This variant goes further: it never knows the product, so the tree is about the account only.)
- **Know the strongest alternative**, not just "nothing": whatever the user sells will be measured against it.
- **Agree on the approach before any product**: which driver, and what must be true to move it.

## Common mistakes
- Generic trees ("revenue = customers × price") instead of their metric names.
- Vague links ("better UX drives cross-sell") and skipped links (product → revenue in one jump).
- Mapping only one way to respond (buying something) instead of the paths they are already on.
- Letting the tool do the thinking. That's why this skill has a Practice mode.

## Why it works
Champions sell internally, in meetings the seller isn't in, and there they must answer one question: what does this do for the number we're graded on? A priority is something executives are already sold on; deals tied to one move, deals that aren't slip down the list. The tree shows where the priorities sit and which role carries them.
