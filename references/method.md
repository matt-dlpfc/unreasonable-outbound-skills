# The method

Our summary of Nate Nasralla's value driver tree, which he also calls the Driver Graph, adapted for outbound. Original: [Value driver trees in sales](https://www.natenasralla.com/resources/value-driver-tree-sales) and the "Driver Graph" one-pager on the same page. Read those for his full explanation; this file is only what the skill needs.

## The tree
A value driver tree puts the outcome a company is graded on at the left and branches it, right to left, into the inputs that produce it. Finance teams already run businesses this way, so drawing one makes you think like the buyer's executives.

**Its job is to be a bridge, not a picture of the financial statements.** It runs from a priority leadership is committed to, down to the metric someone can move. Every link explains how the lower metric produces the higher one, so a change at the bottom has to show up at the top. Nate's own workshop exercise says it best: pick one initiative the buyer's leadership publicly committed to, and trace it down to the metric your product moves.

## Who it's for here: outbound, not a live deal
Nate wrote it for an account executive: a discovery call or another scheduled conversation, where a prospect becomes a champion and the two of them agree on objectives. This skill is for someone doing outbound (cold calls, emails, LinkedIn messages). That changes three things:
- **It's the best *possible* tree, from public information.** That is much less, and much less clear, than what a conversation gives you. Missing figures are normal.
- **It's relevant, not exhaustive.** It has to show where the opportunity lies, and how the seller's product fits the tree: through revenue, operating profit or cost. Branches the seller can't speak to don't need answering.
- **There is no champion or agreed objective yet.** "Owned" means two things: where the seller's product fits the tree, and the role whose remit covers those behaviors, which is who to reach out to.

## The levels

| Level | What it is | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Outcome** | The priority leadership is committed to, with its trade-off and its numeric targets where public ("profitable growth; board: profit > growth; EBITDA 8% → 11%") | Has leadership committed to it (a target, a stated goal, a phrase they repeat)? Does it say how proposals get judged? Last quarter's number alone isn't a priority. |
| **Engines** | The 2–4 things that make the outcome happen: a sketch of how it becomes reality (for a retailer: first orders, repeat orders, and the margin on each) | If you move an engine, does the outcome move? Does it give the seller a mental model of how the buyer thinks about the outcome? |
| **Drivers** | The metrics that make up each engine (new customers ≈ media efficiency × conversion). Deeper levels are often operational or technical. | Can you say how it produces its engine? Could it be measured next quarter? |
| **Behaviors** | What the person responsible for that driver would do differently to move it | Is it a change in practice by their people, with no product name in it? |
| **Pressure** (overlay) | Where something is late, behind, promised or changing | Is it dated and sourced, and on the exact node it hits? |
| **Owned** (overlay) | Where the seller's product fits, and the role whose remit covers those behaviors | Can you point to evidence that the role is responsible for the driver? |

## How each level is built

### Outcome
- **Several priorities usually compete.** Weigh them before choosing (see *Choosing the outcome*).
- **State the trade-off.** "Profitable growth" alone is vague. "Profit > growth" tells you how leadership will judge any proposal.

### Engines
- **They rarely appear in a 10-K.** You build them by thinking about how the business works. The segment table in a filing is a source, not the answer.
- **The exact formula doesn't matter.** What matters is that the engines are actionable and true to how the business makes the outcome.
- **Use their vocabulary where it exists.** Give engine sizes only when they're public; otherwise mark them `?` or leave them out. Don't force them.

### Drivers
- **Each driver is part of its engine's formula,** even a rough one.
- **Give a figure only where it's public.** Otherwise name the driver without one. An unknown value is fine; an unknown mechanism is not.

### Behaviors
- **A behavior is what the owner of that driver would do differently.** Someone is responsible for every driver, and they're thinking "we have to improve this metric". The behavior is the move they could make.
- **Write it as "X, not Y"** when today's practice (Y) is known or can be reasonably guessed ("ask after delivery, not at checkout"). Often it can't, so don't force the form.
- **Go after the dominant cause** when a driver shows one: fit causes 60% of returns, so add size guidance on the top-return SKUs.
- **Behaviors belong to the target's own people.** When the end user is their customer (a developer, an engineer, a shopper), the behavior is what the target's team does for that customer: "put answers where evaluating engineers already look", not "engineers find answers".

### Cross-links
**Branches can talk to each other.** "Shift spend to channels whose buyers repeat" sits under acquisition but serves the repeat engine. Point out such links.

### Pressure
**Put each pressure on the exact node it hits:** peak season sits on site conversion, not on acquisition.

### Owned
Two parts:
- **The fit:** where the seller's product touches the tree, and through which line it reaches the outcome (revenue, operating profit or cost).
- **The role:** whose remit covers the driver those behaviors move. Evidence of remit is a job post or team page describing that work, an org announcement, or the person talking about running it. Presenting a product at an event shows interest, not remit. When remit is inferred, say so.

## Choosing the outcome
Companies rarely have just one priority. The seller can't always move the top one in a meaningful, clear and defensible way. No fixed rule decides this: weigh it. For each of the 2–4 documented priorities:
- **Weight:** how top of mind it is. Do executives repeat it across quarters? Is it in the CEO's letter, or on the first slide of the deck?
- **Size:** how big a part of the business it affects.
- **Fit:** compare the approaches best suited to address that priority with the approaches the seller's product enables. A strong overlap is a fit. A priority whose best approaches are financial, operational or about manufacturing leaves little room, however important it is.

**Combine framings when that's stronger than any single one.** Take a strategic metric for the business line where the seller's wedge is, such as that line's revenue or share in a market leadership is pushing into. Put an operational initiative the company has reported under it, as an engine or driver. Then the top is something the board cares about, and the bridge runs through something they've said they're doing. Use mechanisms the industry knows, even when the company doesn't state them, and tag them [inferred, industry]. For example, in embedded chips, developer adoption drives design-ins, design wins, and then revenue. The seller's impact appears only in the behaviors.

Then choose the framing that balances weight, size and fit best, and say why in a line. Name what you set aside. A small priority with a strong fit is a real choice, but say it's small. If every important priority has a low fit, say the account isn't a priority now, rather than build a bridge to a minor goal.

## The steps
1. **Collect the documented priorities**, in their words: annual report, earnings calls, investor decks, CEO letter. For a private company: funding news, leadership interviews, job posts.
2. **Weigh them and choose the outcome.**
3. **Name the 2–4 engines** that make it happen.
4. **Break the engines on the path into drivers.** Look for metrics they stopped reporting or redefined: that's often where the pain is.
5. **Find the pressure** and tag it on the node it hits. Changes often land on the left while the damage sits several branches to the right. (Estée Lauder, 2020: store closures hit retail sales, but the real damage was in shade confidence online, where shoppers could no longer try a colour.)
6. **Translate drivers into behaviors and mark what's owned.** Trace that behavior back to the outcome and count the links: the further right, the more explicit each link must be.
7. **Map alternative paths:** other ways to move the same outcome, including doing nothing and at least one that isn't buying software. Each has an input it moves, a time to results, a cost, a risk, and an executive story ("a mix story", "an efficiency story", "a transformation story").
8. **Bring it unfinished** and ask "What did I get wrong?" In outbound, the cold questions do this job.

## Rules
- **Their numbers, not yours.** A number from their own earnings call beats your best benchmark.
- **Keep the product off the tree.** If the product shows up, it becomes a pitch and nobody corrects it. (This skill keeps the product in a separate, private *Where we fit* section.)
- **Beat the strongest alternative**, not just "nothing".
- **Agree on the approach before the product**: which driver, and what must be true to move it.

## What good looks like
Nate's worked example (an anonymised D2C home and apparel brand; illustrative figures from a live deal):
- **Outcome:** profitable growth: +15% revenue, EBITDA from 8% to 11%, free cash flow positive. Pressure: "board: profit > growth".
- **Engines:** new customer acquisition (~55% of revenue; ⚠ CAC up 38% in 2 years), repeat customers (45% of revenue, ~70% of profit; ⚠ flat for 6 quarters), and margin per order (~$18 after shipping and returns; ⚠ returns at 24%).
- **Drivers:**
  - acquisition ≈ paid media efficiency (ROAS 2.1x, sliding) × site conversion (mobile 1.4% vs desktop 3.2%; ⚠ peak season in 8 weeks);
  - repeat = 2nd order within 90 days (22%) and loyalty enrollment (9%);
  - margin = average order value ($86, flat) and return rate (fit causes 60% of returns).
- **Behaviors:** one per driver:
  - bid for lifetime value, not cheap clicks;
  - fix mobile checkout;
  - ★ trigger flows by product usage, not by calendar;
  - ★ enroll at the moment of delight (after delivery, not at checkout);
  - bundle in cart, not blanket discounts;
  - fix fit before purchase on the top-return SKUs.
- **Story:** new customers got expensive, so profit now depends on first-time buyers coming back. That puts retention in the hot seat.

Three engines, two drivers each, one behavior per driver. Every node is there because it makes the one above it. In a live deal the two ★ behaviors set the champion and the objectives. In outbound they set where the product fits and who to reach.

## Common mistakes
- **The financial statements redrawn as a tree:** last quarter's result, split into segment revenue and operating expenses. It describes what happened, not how the goal gets produced, and the seller's lever ends up bolted on.
- **A minor goal picked because the seller can reach it,** while the priorities that matter go unmentioned.
- **Links with no mechanism:** if you can't say how a child produces its parent, the tree is a list of related facts.
- **Drivers added to look complete:** a node that no path runs through is noise.
- **Picking the story by formula** ("where importance and performance diverge most"). It doesn't hold up in the real world; use judgment and evidence.
- Generic trees ("revenue = customers × price") instead of their metric names.
- Vague links ("better UX drives cross-sell") and skipped links (product → revenue in one jump).
- Comparing only against yourself.
- Letting the tool do the thinking. That's why this skill has a Practice mode.

## Why it works
Champions sell internally, in meetings the seller isn't in, and there they must answer one question: what does this do for the number we're graded on? A priority is something executives are already sold on; deals tied to one move, while the rest slip down the list. The tree shows where the priorities sit and which role carries them.
